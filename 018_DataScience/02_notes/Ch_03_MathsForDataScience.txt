3.0 INTRODUCTION TO MATHEMATICS IN DATA SCIENCE
============================================================

Mathematics is one of the fundamental foundations of Data Science.

Data Science uses mathematics to:
- Understand data.
- Describe numerical patterns.
- Calculate averages and percentages.
- Measure relationships between variables.
- Build statistical models.
- Understand machine learning algorithms.
- Evaluate model performance.
- Make predictions.

For example, suppose a teacher has the following student
marks:

    Student A = 70
    Student B = 80
    Student C = 90

Mathematics helps us calculate the average marks.

Average = (70 + 80 + 90) / 3

Average = 240 / 3

Average = 80

Therefore, the average score is 80.

A Data Scientist can use this result to understand the
overall performance of the students.

3.1 BASIC ARITHMETIC OPERATIONS
============================================================

Arithmetic is the foundation of mathematical calculations.

------------------------------------------------------------
3.1.1 ADDITION
------------------------------------------------------------

Addition combines two or more numbers.

Example:

    10 + 20 = 30

In Data Science, addition is used to:
- Calculate total sales.
- Add numerical observations.
- Calculate sums.
- Aggregate values.

Example:

A shop sells products worth:

    Monday    = 1000
    Tuesday   = 1500
    Wednesday = 2000

Total sales:

    Total = 1000 + 1500 + 2000

    Total = 4500

Python:

    monday = 1000
    tuesday = 1500
    wednesday = 2000

    total_sales = monday + tuesday + wednesday

    print(total_sales)

Output:

    4500

------------------------------------------------------------
3.1.2 SUBTRACTION
------------------------------------------------------------

Subtraction calculates the difference between numbers.

Example:

    100 - 40 = 60

In Data Science, subtraction is useful for:
- Calculating profit or loss.
- Finding differences between measurements.
- Measuring prediction errors.
- Comparing current and previous values.

Example:

    Revenue = 10000
    Cost = 7000

    Profit = Revenue - Cost

    Profit = 3000

Python:

    revenue = 10000
    cost = 7000

    profit = revenue - cost

    print(profit)

Output:

    3000

------------------------------------------------------------
3.1.3 MULTIPLICATION
------------------------------------------------------------

Multiplication is repeated addition.

Example:

    5 * 4 = 20

In Data Science, multiplication is used for:
- Calculating total costs.
- Scaling values.
- Applying weights in mathematical models.
- Calculating weighted contributions.

Example:

A product costs Rs. 250, and a customer buys 4 units.

    Total Cost = Price * Quantity

    Total Cost = 250 * 4

    Total Cost = Rs. 1000

Python:

    price = 250
    quantity = 4

    total_cost = price * quantity

    print(total_cost)

Output:

    1000

------------------------------------------------------------
3.1.4 DIVISION
------------------------------------------------------------

Division distributes or divides a quantity into parts.

Example:

    20 / 4 = 5

In Data Science, division is used for:
- Calculating averages.
- Calculating percentages.
- Finding ratios.
- Normalizing numerical values.

Example:

A company earns Rs. 5000 from 10 products.

Average revenue per product:

    Average = Total Revenue / Number of Products

    Average = 5000 / 10

    Average = Rs. 500

Python:

    total_revenue = 5000
    number_of_products = 10

    average_revenue = total_revenue / number_of_products

    print(average_revenue)

Output:

    500.0

IMPORTANT:
Always check the denominator before performing division.

Division by zero is undefined in ordinary arithmetic.

In Python:

    print(10 / 0)

This produces a ZeroDivisionError.

------------------------------------------------------------
3.1.5 REMAINDER OR MODULO
------------------------------------------------------------

The modulo operation returns the remainder after division.

Example:

    10 % 3 = 1

Explanation:

    10 = (3 * 3) + 1

The remainder is 1.

Python:

    print(10 % 3)

Output:

    1

Applications:
- Checking whether a number is even or odd.
- Working with repeating patterns.
- Grouping records into batches.
- Implementing cyclic indexing.

Example:

    number = 8

    if number % 2 == 0:
        print("Even")
    else:
        print("Odd")

Output:

    Even

------------------------------------------------------------
3.1.6 EXPONENTIATION
------------------------------------------------------------

Exponentiation raises a number to a power.

Example:

    2^3 = 2 * 2 * 2 = 8

Here:
- 2 is the base.
- 3 is the exponent.
- 8 is the result.

Python uses ** for exponentiation.

    print(2 ** 3)

Output:

    8

Applications:
- Squared errors in machine learning.
- Variance calculations.
- Polynomial expressions.
- Growth calculations.

IMPORTANT PYTHON DIFFERENCE:

    2 ** 3   # Exponentiation: 8

============================================================
3.2 ORDER OF OPERATIONS
============================================================

Mathematical expressions can contain multiple operations.

We must follow a consistent order of operations.

A common convention is PEMDAS:

P = Parentheses
E = Exponents
MD = Multiplication and Division
AS = Addition and Subtraction

Multiplication and division have equal precedence.
Addition and subtraction also have equal precedence.

Operations at the same precedence are generally evaluated
from left to right.

Example 1:

    2 + 3 * 4

Multiplication occurs first.

    = 2 + 12

    = 14

Example 2:

    (2 + 3) * 4

Parentheses occur first.

    = 5 * 4

    = 20

Example 3:

    20 / 5 * 2

Division and multiplication have equal precedence.

Evaluate from left to right:

    = 4 * 2

    = 8

Python:

    print(2 + 3 * 4)
    print((2 + 3) * 4)
    print(20 / 5 * 2)

Output:

    14
    20
    8.0

WHY THIS MATTERS IN DATA SCIENCE
--------------------------------

Incorrect operation ordering can produce incorrect results
in statistical formulas and machine learning calculations.

Always use parentheses when they improve clarity.

For example:

    average = total / count

This is clearer than writing a complicated expression
without grouping the numerator and denominator properly.

3.3 FRACTIONS
============================================================

A fraction represents a part of a whole.

General form:

              Numerator
    Fraction = ---------
             Denominator

Example:

    3/4

Here:
- Numerator = 3
- Denominator = 4

The fraction 3/4 represents three out of four equal parts.

------------------------------------------------------------
3.3.1 FRACTIONS AS DECIMAL NUMBERS
------------------------------------------------------------

A fraction can be converted into a decimal by division.

Example:

    1/2 = 1 / 2 = 0.5

    1/4 = 1 / 4 = 0.25

    3/4 = 3 / 4 = 0.75

Python:

    print(1 / 2)
    print(1 / 4)
    print(3 / 4)

Output:

    0.5
    0.25
    0.75

------------------------------------------------------------
3.3.2 FRACTIONS IN DATA SCIENCE
------------------------------------------------------------

Suppose a dataset contains 100 students.

Out of these students, 25 passed an examination.

The fraction of students who passed is:

    Passed Fraction = 25 / 100

    Passed Fraction = 0.25

The decimal 0.25 can be converted to a percentage:

    Percentage = 0.25 * 100

    Percentage = 25%

Fractions are useful when calculating:
- Proportions.
- Relative frequencies.
- Class distributions.
- Ratios of successful predictions.
- Parts of a dataset.

============================================================
3.4 PERCENTAGES
============================================================

A percentage expresses a quantity out of 100.

The word percentage means "per hundred."

Formula:

                    Part
    Percentage = ----------- * 100
                    Whole

Example:

A student obtains 80 marks out of 100.

    Percentage = (80 / 100) * 100

    Percentage = 80%

Python:

    obtained_marks = 80
    total_marks = 100

    percentage = (
        obtained_marks / total_marks
    ) * 100

    print(percentage)

Output:

    80.0

------------------------------------------------------------
3.4.1 PERCENTAGE EXAMPLE
------------------------------------------------------------

Suppose a student obtains 72 marks out of 90.

    Percentage = (72 / 90) * 100

    Percentage = 80%

Python:

    obtained = 72
    maximum = 90

    percentage = obtained / maximum * 100

    print(percentage)

Output:

    80.0

------------------------------------------------------------
3.4.2 PERCENTAGE INCREASE
------------------------------------------------------------

Percentage increase measures how much a value has increased
relative to its original value.

Formula:

    Percentage Increase =
        ((New Value - Old Value) / Old Value) * 100

Example:

Monthly sales increase from 1000 to 1200 units.

    Increase = 1200 - 1000

    Increase = 200

    Percentage Increase = (200 / 1000) * 100

    Percentage Increase = 20%

Python:

    old_sales = 1000
    new_sales = 1200

    percentage_increase = (
        (new_sales - old_sales) / old_sales
    ) * 100

    print(percentage_increase)

Output:

    20.0

------------------------------------------------------------
3.4.3 PERCENTAGE DECREASE
------------------------------------------------------------

Formula:

    Percentage Decrease =
        ((Old Value - New Value) / Old Value) * 100

Example:

The price of a product decreases from Rs. 500 to Rs. 400.

    Decrease = 500 - 400

    Decrease = 100

    Percentage Decrease = (100 / 500) * 100

    Percentage Decrease = 20%

IMPORTANT:
Percentage change uses the original value as the
denominator.

If the original value is zero, the standard percentage
change formula is undefined.

3.5 RATIOS AND PROPORTIONS
============================================================

------------------------------------------------------------
3.5.1 RATIO
------------------------------------------------------------

A ratio compares two quantities.

Example:

A classroom contains:
- 20 boys.
- 10 girls.

Ratio of boys to girls:

    20 : 10

Simplified ratio:

    2 : 1

This means there are two boys for every girl.

------------------------------------------------------------
3.5.2 PROPORTION
------------------------------------------------------------

A proportion expresses how much one quantity represents
relative to a total or another quantity.

Example:

A dataset contains:
- 60 positive records.
- 40 negative records.

Total records:

    60 + 40 = 100

Positive proportion:

    60 / 100 = 0.60

Negative proportion:

    40 / 100 = 0.40

Therefore:
- Positive proportion = 0.60
- Negative proportion = 0.40

The proportions sum to 1:

    0.60 + 0.40 = 1.00

Their percentages sum to 100%.

    60% + 40% = 100%

------------------------------------------------------------
3.5.3 TRAINING AND TEST DATA
------------------------------------------------------------

In machine learning, a dataset may be divided into:
- Training data.
- Test data.

Suppose there are 1000 records.

If 80% is used for training:

    Training Records = 1000 * 0.80

    Training Records = 800

If 20% is used for testing:

    Test Records = 1000 * 0.20

    Test Records = 200

Python:

    total_records = 1000

    training_ratio = 0.80
    testing_ratio = 0.20

    training_records = (
        total_records * training_ratio
    )

    testing_records = (
        total_records * testing_ratio
    )

    print(training_records)
    print(testing_records)

Output:

    800.0
    200.0

The ratios must sum to 1 when they represent a complete
division of the dataset.

    0.80 + 0.20 = 1.00

A variable is a symbol representing a value that may vary.

Variable:

        20000 + 3000 * experience
    )

    print(predicted_salary)

Output:

    35000

3.8 EQUATIONS
============================================================

An equation states that two expressions are equal.

Example:

    x + 5 = 12

To solve:

    x + 5 = 12

Subtract 5 from both sides:

    x = 12 - 5

Therefore:

    x = 7

------------------------------------------------------------
3.8.1 SOLVING A LINEAR EQUATION
------------------------------------------------------------

Consider:

    2x + 4 = 14

Step 1: Subtract 4 from both sides.

    2x = 10

Step 2: Divide both sides by 2.

    x = 5

Solution:

    x = 5

Verification:

    2(5) + 4 = 14

    10 + 4 = 14

    14 = 14

The solution is correct.

------------------------------------------------------------
3.8.2 EQUATIONS IN DATA SCIENCE
------------------------------------------------------------

Equations represent relationships between variables.

Example:

    Total Cost = Fixed Cost + Variable Cost

    Quantity = 50

Equations are used in:

3.10 EXPONENTS AND POWERS
============================================================

An exponent indicates how many times a base is multiplied
by itself when the exponent is a positive integer.

Example:

    3^4 = 3 * 3 * 3 * 3

    3^4 = 81

Here:
- Base = 3
- Exponent = 4

------------------------------------------------------------
3.10.1 IMPORTANT EXPONENT RULES
------------------------------------------------------------

Rule 1: Multiplication with the same base

    a^m * a^n = a^(m+n)

Example:

    2^3 * 2^2 = 2^5 = 32

Rule 2: Division with the same nonzero base

    a^m / a^n = a^(m-n)

Example:

    2^5 / 2^2 = 2^3 = 8

Rule 3: Power raised to another power

    (a^m)^n = a^(m*n)

Example:

    (2^3)^2 = 2^6 = 64

Rule 4: Power of zero

    a^0 = 1, when a is nonzero

Example:

    5^0 = 1

Rule 5: Negative exponent

    a^(-n) = 1 / a^n, when a is nonzero

Example:

    2^(-3) = 1 / 2^3

    2^(-3) = 1/8

Rule 6: Fractional exponent

    a^(1/2) = square root of a

For nonnegative real a:

    9^(1/2) = 3

------------------------------------------------------------
3.10.2 EXPONENTS IN DATA SCIENCE
------------------------------------------------------------

Exponents appear in:
- Squared error calculations.
- Variance.
- Polynomial features.
- Exponential growth models.
- Probability distributions.
- Machine learning mathematics.

Example:

Suppose the actual value is 10 and the predicted value
is 7.

Prediction error:

    Error = Actual - Predicted

    Error = 10 - 7

    Error = 3

Squared error:

    Squared Error = Error^2

    Squared Error = 3^2

    Squared Error = 9

Python:

    actual = 10
    predicted = 7

    error = actual - predicted
    squared_error = error ** 2

    print(error)
    print(squared_error)

Output:

    3
    9

Squared errors are used in many regression evaluation
methods.

3.11 ROOTS
============================================================

A root reverses the operation of raising a number to a
power, when the relevant root is defined.

------------------------------------------------------------
3.11.1 SQUARE ROOT
------------------------------------------------------------

The square root of a nonnegative number is its
nonnegative square root.

Example:

    sqrt(25) = 5

Because:

    5 * 5 = 25

Other examples:

    sqrt(9) = 3
    sqrt(16) = 4
    sqrt(100) = 10

Python:

    import math

    print(math.sqrt(25))
    print(math.sqrt(9))

Output:

    5.0
    3.0

------------------------------------------------------------
3.11.2 CUBE ROOT
------------------------------------------------------------

The cube root of a number is a value that, when cubed,
gives the original number.

Example:

    Cube Root of 27 = 3

Because:

    3^3 = 27

The real cube root of a negative number is also defined.

Example:

    Cube Root of -8 = -2

Python:

    print(27 ** (1 / 3))

This computes an approximate floating-point result.

For negative inputs, be careful with fractional powers
in Python because they may not return the real cube root.

------------------------------------------------------------
3.11.3 ROOTS IN DATA SCIENCE
------------------------------------------------------------

Roots appear in:
- Standard deviation.
- Euclidean distance.
- Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE).
- Geometric calculations.

Example:

Suppose the squared differences between actual and
predicted values have a mean of 16.

Then:

    RMSE = sqrt(16)

    RMSE = 4

RMSE will be studied more fully when learning evaluation
metrics.

3.12 LOGARITHMS
============================================================

A logarithm answers the question:

"To what power must a base be raised to obtain a number?"

General relationship:

    If b^x = y, then log_b(y) = x

Here:
- b is the base.
- y is the number.
- x is the logarithm.

Example:

    2^3 = 8

Therefore:

    log_2(8) = 3

------------------------------------------------------------
3.12.1 COMMON LOGARITHM
------------------------------------------------------------

A common logarithm uses base 10.

Example:

    log_10(100) = 2

Because:

    10^2 = 100

Another example:

    log_10(1000) = 3

Because:

    10^3 = 1000

------------------------------------------------------------
3.12.2 NATURAL LOGARITHM
------------------------------------------------------------

The natural logarithm uses the mathematical constant e
as its base.

The constant e is approximately:

    e = 2.71828

The natural logarithm is written as:

    ln(x)

Example:

    ln(e) = 1

Because:

    e^1 = e

Python:

    import math

    print(math.log(100))
    print(math.log10(100))
    print(math.log(1000, 10))

Output:

    4.605170185988092
    2.0
    3.0

IMPORTANT PYTHON DETAIL:

    math.log(x)

calculates the natural logarithm by default.

    math.log10(x)

calculates the base-10 logarithm.

------------------------------------------------------------
3.12.3 LOGARITHM RULES
------------------------------------------------------------

For positive values in the relevant domains:

Rule 1:

    log(a * b) = log(a) + log(b)

Rule 2:

    log(a / b) = log(a) - log(b)

Rule 3:

    log(a^n) = n * log(a)

The same logarithm base must be used consistently in
these identities.

------------------------------------------------------------
3.12.4 LOGARITHMS IN DATA SCIENCE
------------------------------------------------------------

Logarithms are useful for:
- Transforming highly skewed data.
- Analyzing exponential growth.
- Understanding information theory.
- Working with probabilities.
- Analyzing algorithmic complexity.
- Studying certain machine learning loss functions.

Example:

Suppose sales grow exponentially over time.

A logarithmic transformation may help analyze the
relationship between time and sales.

However, logarithms of real numbers require positive
inputs.

For example:

    log(0)

is undefined.

    log(-5)

is not defined as a real-valued natural logarithm.

For data containing zero or negative values, a suitable
transformation requires additional care.

3.13 ABSOLUTE VALUE
============================================================

Absolute value measures the distance of a number from
zero on the number line.

It is never negative.

Examples:

    |5| = 5
    |-5| = 5
    |0| = 0

Python:

    print(abs(5))
    print(abs(-5))
    print(abs(0))

Output:

    5
    5
    0

------------------------------------------------------------
3.13.1 ABSOLUTE ERROR
------------------------------------------------------------

Suppose:
- Actual value = 100
- Predicted value = 90

Signed error:

    Error = Actual - Predicted

    Error = 100 - 90

    Error = 10

Absolute error:

    Absolute Error = |Actual - Predicted|

    Absolute Error = |100 - 90|

    Absolute Error = 10

Now suppose the prediction is 110.

    Error = 100 - 110

    Error = -10

    Absolute Error = |-10|

    Absolute Error = 10

Absolute error ignores the direction of the error.

Python:

    actual = 100

    prediction_1 = 90
    prediction_2 = 110

    error_1 = abs(actual - prediction_1)
    error_2 = abs(actual - prediction_2)

    print(error_1)
    print(error_2)

Output:

    10
    10

Absolute error is useful for evaluating numerical
predictions.

3.14 SUMMATION NOTATION
============================================================

Summation notation is a compact mathematical way to
represent the addition of multiple values.

The Greek letter sigma is written as:

    Σ

It means "sum."

Example:

    x1 + x2 + x3 + x4

can be represented as:

    Σ xi, for i = 1 to 4

This means:

    x1 + x2 + x3 + x4

------------------------------------------------------------
3.14.1 EXAMPLE
------------------------------------------------------------

Suppose:

    x1 = 10
    x2 = 20
    x3 = 30

Then:

    Σ xi, for i = 1 to 3

means:

    10 + 20 + 30

    = 60

Python:

    values = [10, 20, 30]

    total = sum(values)

    print(total)

Output:

    60

------------------------------------------------------------
3.14.2 SUMMATION IN DATA SCIENCE
------------------------------------------------------------

Suppose a dataset contains n observations:

    x1, x2, x3, ..., xn

Their sum is:

           n
    Sum =  Σ xi
          i=1

The arithmetic mean is:

                n
                Σ xi
               i=1
    Mean = -------------
                   n

In simple terms:

    Mean = Sum of all values / Number of values

Example:

    Values = [10, 20, 30, 40]

    Sum = 100

    Count = 4

    Mean = 100 / 4

    Mean = 25

Python:

    values = [10, 20, 30, 40]

    mean = sum(values) / len(values)

    print(mean)

Output:

    25.0

3.15 BASIC SET THEORY
============================================================

Set theory is a branch of mathematics that studies
collections of distinct elements.

A set is a collection of distinct objects.

Example:

    A = {1, 2, 3, 4}

Here, A is a set containing four elements.

Sets are useful in:
- Data filtering.
- Removing duplicate values.
- Comparing groups.
- Understanding class labels.
- Database operations.
- Mathematical reasoning.

------------------------------------------------------------
3.15.1 ELEMENTS OF A SET
------------------------------------------------------------

Suppose:

    A = {1, 2, 3}

Then:
- 1 belongs to A.
- 2 belongs to A.
- 4 does not belong to A.

Mathematical notation:

    2 ∈ A

means 2 is an element of A.

    4 ∉ A

means 4 is not an element of A.

Python:

    A = {1, 2, 3}

    print(2 in A)
    print(4 in A)

Output:

    True
    False

------------------------------------------------------------
3.15.2 UNION
------------------------------------------------------------

Union combines the elements of two sets.

Example:

    A = {1, 2, 3}
    B = {3, 4, 5}

    A ∪ B = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5}

Python:

    A = {1, 2, 3}
    B = {3, 4, 5}

    print(A | B)

Output:

    {1, 2, 3, 4, 5}

The order of set elements in Python output is not
guaranteed.

------------------------------------------------------------
3.15.3 INTERSECTION
------------------------------------------------------------

Intersection contains the elements common to both sets.

Example:

    A = {1, 2, 3}
    B = {3, 4, 5}

    A ∩ B = {3}

Python:

    print(A & B)

Output:

    {3}

------------------------------------------------------------
3.15.4 DIFFERENCE
------------------------------------------------------------

Difference contains the elements in one set that are
not in the other.

Example:

    A = {1, 2, 3}
    B = {3, 4, 5}

    A - B = {1, 2}

Python:

    print(A - B)

Output:

    {1, 2}

3.16 FUNCTIONS
============================================================

A mathematical function represents a rule that maps each
input in its domain to exactly one output.

A function is often written as:

    y = f(x)

Here:
- x is the input.
- f represents the rule.
- y is the output.

Example:

    f(x) = 2x + 3

If x = 4:

    f(4) = 2(4) + 3

    f(4) = 8 + 3

    f(4) = 11

------------------------------------------------------------
3.16.1 INPUT AND OUTPUT
------------------------------------------------------------

Consider the function:

    f(x) = x^2

Input:

    x = 2

Output:

    f(2) = 2^2 = 4

Another input:

    x = 5

Output:

    f(5) = 5^2 = 25

Python:

    def square(x):
        return x ** 2

    print(square(2))
    print(square(5))

Output:

    4
    25

------------------------------------------------------------
3.16.2 DOMAIN AND RANGE
------------------------------------------------------------

Domain:
The set of allowed input values for a function.

Range:
The set of output values produced by the function for
the inputs in its domain.

Example:

    f(x) = x^2

If the domain is:

    {-2, -1, 0, 1, 2}

The corresponding outputs are:

    {4, 1, 0, 1, 4}

The distinct output values form the range:

    {0, 1, 4}

For a function over all real numbers:

    f(x) = x^2

the domain is all real numbers and the range is all
nonnegative real numbers.

3.16.3 FUNCTIONS IN DATA SCIENCE
------------------------------------------------------------

Functions are used to describe relationships.

Example:

    Total Price = Price * Quantity

This can be represented as:

    total_price(price, quantity) = price * quantity

Python:

    def total_price(price, quantity):
        return price * quantity

    print(total_price(100, 5))

Output:

    500

Functions are important in:
- Mathematical modeling.
- Data transformations.
- Feature engineering.
- Statistical calculations.
- Machine learning algorithms.

    x < 0, y > 0

    x < 0, y < 0

Points on the axes are not inside any of the four

3.18 BASIC GRAPHS AND MATHEMATICAL RELATIONSHIPS
============================================================

Graphs provide a visual representation of mathematical
relationships.

------------------------------------------------------------
3.18.1 LINEAR RELATIONSHIP
------------------------------------------------------------

A basic linear equation is:

    y = mx + b

Where:
- x = input variable.
- y = output variable.
- m = slope.
- b = y-intercept.

Example:

    y = 2x + 1

If x = 0:

    y = 2(0) + 1 = 1

If x = 1:

    y = 2(1) + 1 = 3

If x = 2:

    y = 2(2) + 1 = 5

Table:

    x    y
    --------
    0    1
    1    3
    2    5
    3    7

The output increases by 2 whenever x increases by 1.

------------------------------------------------------------
3.18.2 SLOPE
------------------------------------------------------------

Slope measures the change in y relative to the change
in x between two points.

Formula:

             y2 - y1
    Slope = ---------
             x2 - x1

provided that x2 is not equal to x1.

Example:

    Point 1 = (1, 3)
    Point 2 = (3, 7)

Then:

    Slope = (7 - 3) / (3 - 1)

    Slope = 4 / 2

    Slope = 2

This means y increases by 2 for every one-unit increase
in x along this line.

------------------------------------------------------------
3.18.3 POSITIVE RELATIONSHIP
------------------------------------------------------------

A positive relationship means larger values of one
variable tend to be associated with larger values of
another variable.

Example:

    Study Hours and Examination Marks

More study hours may be associated with higher marks.

However, this is not guaranteed for every student.

Other factors may influence the result.

------------------------------------------------------------
3.18.4 NEGATIVE RELATIONSHIP
------------------------------------------------------------

A negative relationship means larger values of one
variable tend to be associated with smaller values of
another variable.

Example:

    Product Price and Demand

In some markets, a higher price may be associated with
lower demand.

Actual relationships depend on the market and other
conditions.

------------------------------------------------------------
3.18.5 NO CLEAR RELATIONSHIP
------------------------------------------------------------

Sometimes two variables do not show an obvious
relationship.

Example:

    Shoe Size and Computer Password Length

There is generally no meaningful direct relationship
between these quantities.

IMPORTANT:
An apparent relationship between two variables does
not automatically prove that one causes the other.

3.19 DISTANCE BETWEEN TWO POINTS
============================================================

The Euclidean distance measures straight-line distance
between two points.

For points:

    P = (x1, y1)
    Q = (x2, y2)

The distance formula is:

    Distance = sqrt(
        (x2 - x1)^2 + (y2 - y1)^2
    )

------------------------------------------------------------
3.19.1 EXAMPLE
------------------------------------------------------------

Suppose:

    P = (1, 2)
    Q = (4, 6)

Calculate the coordinate differences:

    Difference in x = 4 - 1 = 3

    Difference in y = 6 - 2 = 4

Square the differences:

    3^2 = 9

    4^2 = 16

Add the squared differences:

    9 + 16 = 25

Take the square root:

    Distance = sqrt(25)

    Distance = 5

Python:

    import math

    x1, y1 = 1, 2
    x2, y2 = 4, 6

    distance = math.sqrt(
        (x2 - x1) ** 2
        + (y2 - y1) ** 2
    )

    print(distance)

Output:

    5.0

------------------------------------------------------------
3.19.2 DISTANCE IN DATA SCIENCE
------------------------------------------------------------

Distance is used in:
- Geographical calculations.
- Similarity analysis.
- Clustering algorithms.
- Nearest-neighbor methods.
- Comparing numerical feature vectors.

For example, a nearest-neighbor algorithm may compare
the distance between a new observation and existing
observations.

However, features with different scales can affect
distance calculations. Scaling may be necessary.

    marks = [80, 75, 90, 85, 70]

3.22 MATHEMATICS MISTAKES TO AVOID
============================================================

Mistake 1: Incorrect percentage formula

Incorrect:

    obtained_marks / 100

This is only correct as a proportion when the maximum
possible marks are exactly 100.

General formula:

    percentage = (
        obtained_marks / maximum_possible_marks
    ) * 100

Mistake 2: Confusing multiplication and exponentiation

    2 * 3 = 6

    2 ** 3 = 8

Mistake 3: Forgetting parentheses

Compare:

    a + b / c

with:

    (a + b) / c

These expressions may produce different results.

Mistake 4: Dividing by zero

Always check that the denominator is nonzero when the
formula requires division.

Mistake 5: Confusing percentage and percentage points

If a rate rises from 20% to 30%:

    Percentage-point increase = 30% - 20%

    = 10 percentage points

Relative percentage increase:

    ((30 - 20) / 20) * 100

    = 50%

These are different measurements.

Mistake 6: Treating correlation as causation

Two variables may move together without one directly
causing the other.

Mistake 7: Ignoring numerical precision

Computers often represent decimal numbers approximately
using floating-point arithmetic.

For example:

    print(0.1 + 0.2)

may display:

    0.30000000000000004

This is a normal floating-point representation issue.

Mistake 8: Assuming every mathematical function accepts
every possible input

Examples:

    math.sqrt(-1)

does not return a real number using the standard real
math.sqrt function.

Similarly, real logarithms require positive inputs.

Mistake 9: Confusing mathematical notation with Python

Mathematics:

    x^2

Python:

    x ** 2

In Python, ^ is a bitwise XOR operator, not exponentiation.

Mistake 10: Using averages without considering the data

An average can hide differences between observations.

For example:

    [10, 10, 10, 10, 100]

The average is:

    140 / 5 = 28

But four of the five values are 10.

This illustrates why Data Science also requires statistics
and visual exploration.
